Engineering with AI, not simply using AI

AI-assisted engineering requires more than giving developers access to coding assistants. Organizations must reconsider workflows, engineering standards, validation, human oversight, and the skills needed to deliver reliable software.

I have worked on this from both sides: creating standards and driving adoption in a global enterprise, and building curriculum that prepares the next generation of engineers for AI-native development.

How I approach it

The principles that guide my enterprise work and my teaching. These describe my engineering philosophy rather than specific organizational results.

AI-native engineering practices

AI can contribute across the entire software lifecycle, not just code generation. Requirements, technical planning, software architecture, implementation, testing, review, and production readiness each benefit from AI, and each needs its own practices for using it well. Treating AI as a participant in the whole workflow, rather than as autocomplete, changes how teams plan and divide work.

Repeatability and organizational consistency

When every team invents its own way of working with AI, quality becomes unpredictable. Shared standards make responsible use the default, make results comparable across teams, and give engineers a clear understanding of what is expected of them.

Quality, validation, and human oversight

  • Validation beyond reading generated code. Manual review alone does not scale with the volume AI can produce.
  • Automated testing and deterministic checks. Tests, type systems, linters, and CI gates verify behavior regardless of who or what wrote the code.
  • Evaluation and verification of model output. Outputs should be assessed systematically, not only trusted or rejected case by case.
  • Human-in-the-loop and human-on-the-loop. The right level of oversight depends on the risk of the task, and teams should choose it deliberately.
  • Security, data protection, and responsible use. Clear guidance on what data and systems AI tools may touch.
  • Explicit ownership and accountability. Engineers remain accountable for every change they ship, however it was produced.

Developing professional judgment

As implementation becomes increasingly automated, the most valuable engineering skills shift toward problem framing, architecture, critical evaluation, and judgment. Training and coaching should build those skills deliberately, for working engineers and students alike.
